National Statistics · Japan

Japan’s population has fallen for 15 straight years.

It peaked at 128.1 million in 2008. Since then every year has brought fewer births, more deaths, and a population that is older than any other on Earth — and yet the economy, the cities and daily life keep adapting.

One story runs through every chart on this page: a shrinking, aging, resilient nation. All figures are real published statistics, rounded, with their source under each chart. Projections are shown dashed.

02 · Population & Age Structure

The pyramid became a tower

Pick a year and watch the age pyramid flip: wide at the bottom in 1950, top-heavy by 2025, and nearly a column by 2070.

03 · Births, Deaths & Longevity

Fewer babies, longer lives

Births have halved since their 1973 peak while deaths keep climbing — yet a newborn today can expect to live to 84.

The arithmetic of decline

In 2024 Japan recorded about 720,000 births and about 1,620,000 deaths — a natural decrease of roughly 900,000 people in one year, the largest on record. Net migration (about +340,000 in 2024) softens the fall but does not reverse it.

At the same time, Japan has the world’s longest life expectancy: 87.1 years for women and 81.1 for men (2024). The country is not dying young — it is living longer and having far fewer children.

Fertility has been below the 2.1 replacement level since 1975. At 1.15 children per woman (2024), each generation is roughly half the size of the one before it.

Source: MHLW vital statistics; Statistics Bureau of Japan (e-Stat); Immigration Services Agency, 2024
04 · Economy

Big, rich, barely growing

The world’s fourth-largest economy has grown by less than 1% a year on average since 2000 — but unemployment has stayed near 2.5% through every shock.

Why growth is so slow — and why it matters less than it looks

With the working-age population shrinking by about 0.5% a year, even flat total GDP means rising GDP per worker. Japan’s GDP per capita (about $33,800 in 2024) is still among the highest in Asia.

Two shocks define the century: the global financial crisis (−5.4% in 2009) and the pandemic (−4.3% in 2020). Both were followed by recoveries — but never a return to the fast growth of the 1980s.

After decades of deflation, prices rose again: CPI inflation averaged 2.7% in 2024, and the Bank of Japan ended its negative-rate policy the same year.

Source: Cabinet Office, national accounts; IMF World Economic Outlook 2024; Bank of Japan
05 · Labour & Society

More women working, more foreign neighbours

The labour force adapted to shrinkage: women stayed in work after having children, and foreign residents more than tripled since 2000.

The quiet adaptation

Female labour-force participation for ages 25–29 rose from 72% in 1990 to 88% in 2024, and the old dip at ages 30–34 — women leaving work to raise children — has largely flattened. Childcare expansion and a tight labour market did what policy speeches could not.

Foreign residents reached 3.77 million at the end of 2024 (3.0% of the population), up from about 1.1 million in 2000. China and Viet Nam are the two largest groups.

Three in four workers are now in services; manufacturing employs about one in four, and farming, forestry and fishing just 3%.

Source: Statistics Bureau of Japan, Labour Force Survey 2024; Immigration Services Agency, 2024
06 · Regions

Tokyo grows younger; Akita grows older

Aging is not evenly spread. The capital region stays young and keeps growing, while rural prefectures lose more than 1% of their people every year.
